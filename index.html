Closed-loop peristaltic pump with water-level control

Developed an automated peristaltic pumping system for electronically controlled fluid transfer and water-level regulation.

Role
Control logic, electrical architecture, system integration
Hardware
STM32 H5 Nucleo · TMC2209 · stepper · LCD · keypad
Fabrication
3D printing · laser cutting

01Problem

Hold a target water level without manual pump operation. The system has to sense the level, decide when to pump, drive a peristaltic pump, and let a user monitor and set it, all in one self-contained unit.

02Approach
closed-loop architecture

03Design

Control

Implemented closed-loop logic on the STM32: water-level feedback decides when the pump actuates.

Actuation

Drove a stepper-powered peristaltic pump through a TMC2209 driver. In a peristaltic pump only the tubing touches the fluid.

Power & safety

Designed the electrical architecture around a 24 V supply with DC-DC conversion for each subsystem’s voltage; added an emergency switch.

Interface

Integrated an LCD for system status and a keypad for user input.

04Build

  • Integrated tubing, pump mechanism, level sensor, electronics and fluid reservoirs into a single physical prototype.
  • Fabricated structural components with 3D printing and laser cutting.

05Testing & iteration

Verified end-to-end operation: level feedback triggered pump actuation automatically, and the LCD and keypad interface worked as intended.

Limitation found → next iteration

The water-level sensor showed corrosion and reliability issues in use. A version 2 should prioritize level sensing suited to long-term water exposure, the weakest link in the loop.

06Results

Closed-loop regulation
Water level held automatically through pump actuation
Autonomous transfer
Driven by real-time level feedback, no manual pumping
Working interface
LCD status display and keypad input demonstrated
Integrated prototype
Mechanical, electrical, sensing, code and UI in one unit

07Takeaway

Mechatronic system integration: sensor, microcontroller, motor driver, power electronics and mechanical hardware working as one closed loop, plus the judgment to find the least reliable component and target it next.

DMLS-printed 316L stainless-steel lattices: tension and compression

Designed, printed and tested 316L lattice structures to characterize stiffness, strength and failure behavior for lightweight, energy-absorbing applications.

Process
DMLS metal powder-bed fusion
Testing
Instron UTM · tension and compression
Analysis
Force–displacement → engineering stress–strain

01Problem

Metal AM lattices can make light, energy-absorbing structures, but their stiffness and strength depend on geometry and print quality. Goal: measure stiffness, yield and ultimate strength in tension and compression, and identify how the lattices fail.

02Approach

03Design decisions

Orientation45° (+X) and 15° (+Z)
ScanStrategic scan patterns
SupportsStabilized support structures
MaterialSS316L powder, DMLS

05Testing

  • Tested 5 tensile and 5 compression specimens on an Instron UTM.
  • Converted raw force–displacement data into engineering stress–strain curves to compare samples.
  • Characterized failure: strut tearing in tension, progressive buckling in compression.

06Results

Load caseStrengthModulusFailure mode
Tension21–23 MPa UTS2.4–2.5 GPaStrut tearing
Compression22–23 MPa0.44–1.50 GPaProgressive buckling

Tensile properties were stable and repeatable, and compressive strength stayed steady even as compressive modulus varied with lattice buckling. The result is a controlled, non-brittle failure profile suited to energy absorption.

07Takeaway

The metal-AM qualification loop at small scale: build-process decisions, standardized mechanical testing, data reduction and failure-mode interpretation, the same workflow used to decide whether an AM part is fit for service.
